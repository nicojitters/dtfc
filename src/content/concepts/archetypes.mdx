## What are Archetypes?

Archetypes are universal constructs we are born with, like instinct and genetic patterning. Our life experiences activate an archetype while many others stay dormant. We meet them in story and dream — and we also meet people who embody an archetype.

## Character Archetypes

Character archetypes include: Mother and Father (both Good and Bad), Simpleton (youngest or least socialized child), Old Man or Woman, Stepmother and Stepfather, King and Queen, Fairy Godmother, Witch, Fairy, Animal (Dragon, Bear, Horse, Dolphin, Eagle), Friend, Troll, Goblin, Elf, Warrior, Devil, Saint, Bully, Angel, Giant, Magician (both Good and Bad), Trickster, Hunter.

All these characters are present inside each of us, awaiting awakening in all their ambiguity.

## Archetypal Situations

First sentences in a story, play, or film establish the archetypal situation:

- Mother/Queen/Father/King is sick or dead.
- Stepmother, missing father.
- Family is poor.
- Something has been stolen or gone missing.
- Someone needs to marry.
- There is a famine or other destitution that needs healing.
- An other-reality character appears and disturbs the community.

## One Story — The Archetype of Plot

Every story shares one underlying structure: the **Wayfarer’s Journey**. The wayfarer leaves the current situation, finds their way through a series of adventures and learnings, and returns home. The intellectual interest is in *how* this version of the story unfolds; the healing is in the heart’s reassurance as the old pattern works out once again.

The first function of story is to reinforce or restore hope. Both children and adults often feel they are in seemingly hopeless situations — stories are gifts from our ancestors to comfort us and teach us what to do when we feel hopeless.

Each person’s life is also structured as an earthly journey from birth to death, with many individual stories occurring along the way. Even a single day can follow the one story structure.

## Archetypes in Performance

Once an audience enters the story and recognizes the characters, the players must maintain the magic. Archetypes capture audience members so completely that they are transported — *in* the world of the play, not watching it from outside. A sloppy or ego-centered performance can destroy the fragile archetypal world, and audiences sense it immediately.

When children act out the story on the playground afterward, and give the characters new adventures — that is a permanent gift.
