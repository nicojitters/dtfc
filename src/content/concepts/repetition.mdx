## Repetition and the Four R&rsquo;s

If we had &ldquo;Four R&rsquo;s&rdquo; of Oral Tradition, Repetition would be a key listening and memory aid. The other three would be Rhythm, Rhyme, and Resonance.

Both Shakespeare and DT:FC Children&rsquo;s Scripts use repetition:

- in **repeated incidents** (three siblings, first two fail the first challenge)
- in **repeated words and phrases** that emphasize character and place name and qualities (&ldquo;the evil queen,&rdquo; &ldquo;the hedge of thorns,&rdquo; the chorus of a song)
- in **repeated main concepts** (courage, resilience, patience)

Shakespeare was raised in a culture where most people did not read or write. To call them illiterate is correct only about those two skills. Those same people were able to hear a story once and repeat all or parts of it, duplicating rhythm and rhyme. Many people could tell traditional stories (see Geoffrey Chaucer&rsquo;s *Canterbury Tales* for examples). Whole parts of religious texts were in their memory, often in Latin. Today, jokes that tell a long story with a punchline are part of this tradition.

The concept of repetition is familiar to school teachers. Repetition is used in scripts to emphasize. For example, when a new character is introduced the script has the character&rsquo;s name and qualities repeated by various Players. The main theme of the play or a scene may be repeated several times, often using different words emphasizing the concept.

## How Repetition Functions in Storytelling

In oral narrative traditions, repetition serves multiple sophisticated purposes beyond simple memorization. Traditional storytellers employ repetition in several strategic ways:

**Structural Repetition:** Key phrases, refrains, and formulaic expressions create a scaffold that supports both the storyteller&rsquo;s memory and the audience&rsquo;s comprehension. When the same phrase is repeated on three or more occasions during the telling of a story, listeners can move through three stages: repeat after teacher, say with teacher, say without teacher &mdash; providing scaffolding that supports the learning of collocations. ([British Council TeachingEnglish](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/articles/rhythm-rhyme-repetition-reasoning-and))

**Thematic Reinforcement:** Repeating key information or using recurring motifs helps reinforce important concepts and makes them easier to remember. ([Vim and Vibe Productions](https://vimandvibe.com/blog/storytelling-memory-retention/)) This technique allows complex cultural knowledge to be embedded within accessible narrative frameworks.

**Symbolic Encoding:** Repetition reinforces the message, making it easier to remember, while symbolic language allows for the encoding of complex meanings within simple verses &mdash; enabling the transmission of complex ideas without requiring extensive literacy. ([Native Tribe Info](https://nativetribe.info/native-american-oral-history-memory-keeping/))

## Educational Applications and Reading Aloud

The connection between oral tradition and modern learning practices is particularly evident in reading aloud. Research shows that reading out loud provides:

- enhanced vocabulary and language skills through contextual learning and pronunciation practice
- strengthened memory by engaging multiple senses for better retention and recall
- increased confidence in public speaking

([7 Benefits of Reading Out Loud | Listening.com](https://www.listening.com/blog/benefits-of-reading-out-loud))

## Cultural and Developmental Significance

Oral storytelling traditions recognize repetition&rsquo;s role in holistic education. Stories develop listening skills, memory, and imagination, and they support social and emotional learning to develop the whole child. ([Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian](https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/informational/storytelling-and-oral-traditions)) This multifaceted approach demonstrates how repetition in oral tradition serves not just as a memory aid but as a comprehensive educational tool.

The adaptability of oral tradition also sets it apart from written forms. Oral narratives can change with each performance, allowing for adaptability ([Greek Mythology Worldwide](https://greek.mythologyworldwide.com/homers-use-of-repetition-a-tool-for-emphasis-and-memory/)) while maintaining core structural elements through repetition.
