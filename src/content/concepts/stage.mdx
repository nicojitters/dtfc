The Stage is more than a physical location — it&rsquo;s the relationship between Players and Audience. Each configuration creates a different kind of intimacy, changes what sight lines are possible, and shapes where Players can enter and exit. Knowing the stage you&rsquo;re working with is the first step toward fearless staging.

## Proscenium

<StageDiagram variant="proscenium" />

The Proscenium is the most familiar configuration: the Audience sits in forward-facing seating while the Stage is framed in front of them. An apron separates the Audience from the Stage, and that apron area can also seat an orchestra.

**Usually found in:**

- Auditoriums
- Theatre Buildings
- Opera Houses
- Outdoor Theatres with fixed seating

Players enter from the back and sides, depending on the set design.

## Arena

<StageDiagram variant="arena" />

In Arena staging, the Audience sits on all four sides facing the Stage. A semi-formal apron encloses the Stage, and there are usually four set aisles at the corners for Players to enter and exit.

**Usually found in:**

- Theatre Buildings
- Sports Gymnasiums

Players enter from the back and sides, depending on the set design.

## Theatre in the Round <span class="bg-clay-500/10 text-clay-500 ml-2 rounded px-2 py-0.5 text-xs font-medium tracking-wide uppercase">DT:FC Favorite</span>

<StageDiagram variant="in-the-round" />

Theatre in the Round places the Audience all the way around the Stage, with an informal or open circular space at the center. It&rsquo;s the DT:FC Favorite because it creates immediate intimacy — every seat is close, every face is visible, and the energy flows in all directions.

**Usually formed in:**

- Multi-Purpose Spaces
- Gymnasiums
- Conference Rooms

Players enter from the back and sides; sometimes they are seated in the Audience before stepping into the playing area.

## Thrust

<StageDiagram variant="thrust" />

The Thrust Stage extends the playing area forward into the Audience seating. Audience members sit on two sides facing the Thrust extension, while the back of the Stage remains a backing wall or set. There is no formal apron area.

Players enter from the back, sides, and aisles — the extended stage makes aisle entrances especially dynamic.

## Unusual Configurations

<StageDiagram variant="unusual" />

Not every performance happens in a dedicated theatre. Unusual configurations embrace wherever Players find a performance space.

**Multi-focus:** The playing area is distributed across various locations — for example, at horizontal ends of a rectangle or on a balcony. The Audience&rsquo;s attention shifts to wherever the Players are active.

**Found Spaces:** Players identify a performance space within an existing environment and make it work — a hallway, a courtyard, a lobby.

**Touring Companies** adapt their staging to each venue they visit.

**Pop-up Events** create temporary performance spaces in unexpected settings.

**Street performers** bring theatre directly into public life, with no separation between everyday space and playing space.

## Sight Lines

<StageDiagram variant="sightlines" />

&ldquo;Sight Lines&rdquo; are the lines of vision connecting each Audience member to the Players — and the Players back to the Audience. Every configuration creates its own sight-line possibilities and limitations.

**Key principles:**

- The Audience must be able to see everything at all times — staging choices that block sight lines break the connection.
- Players and Audience both adapt to the configuration; awareness of where you are in relation to every seat matters.
- Exits and entrances depend on the configuration — understanding sight lines helps Players move cleanly and intentionally.

When staging a scene, the Facilitator walks the space from multiple vantage points before the Players arrive, so every sight-line choice is intentional.
