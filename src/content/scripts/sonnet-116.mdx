## About this poem

Sonnet 116 is not a stage speech but a lyric poem — one of the most complete statements in the language on the nature of love. It defines love by what it is not: love that changes is not love; love that bends when its object changes is not love; love that yields to time is not love. The positive definition emerges only in the negative space, but it arrives by the final couplet with full force.

Ideal for Players exploring the relationship between thought and breath, or for ensemble work in which the sonnet is divided across multiple voices. Excellent as a warm-up text — fourteen lines that contain a complete argument.

*From SHAKE-SPEARES SONNETS, 1609.*

## Poem

Let me not to the marriage of true minds
Admit impediments; love is not love
Which alters when it alteration finds,
Or bends with the remover to remove.
O no, it is an ever-fixèd mark
That looks on tempests and is never shaken;
It is the star to every wand&rsquo;ring bark
Whose worth&rsquo;s unknown, although his height be taken.
Love&rsquo;s not time&rsquo;s fool, though rosy lips and cheeks
Within his bending sickle&rsquo;s compass come.
Love alters not with his brief hours and weeks,
But bears it out even to the edge of doom:
If this be error and upon me proved,
I never writ, nor no man ever loved.

## Facilitator Notes

**Warm-up:** Ask Players to define one abstract concept — courage, fairness, friendship — only by saying what it is NOT. Then read the sonnet.

**Debrief prompts:**
- The sonnet defines love almost entirely in negatives: "love is not love which alters," "love&rsquo;s not time&rsquo;s fool." Why might a poet choose to define something by its failures rather than its substance?
- The "ever-fixèd mark" and the "star" are navigational images. What does it mean that love is something sailors steer by?
- The final couplet makes a bold claim: if I&rsquo;m wrong about any of this, then nothing I&rsquo;ve written exists and no one has ever loved. How does a Player land that claim without it feeling like a boast?
- Try the sonnet as a group — one Player per line, or one Player per quatrain. What changes when the argument is shared?
